FanCrew

Turning fragmented creative hiring into one connected workflow

FanCrew is a creator-focused marketplace that connects Creators with creative professionals — "Crew." For MVP 1, the goal was to make the entire relationship between a Creator and Crew member manageable inside one product — from discovering talent and posting a project to hiring, collaboration, revisions, approval and payment.

01 — The Challenge

Hiring creative talent is rarely one single task.

For a Creator, finding a designer, editor, photographer or other creative professional often means moving between multiple disconnected tools.

Find someone → Check portfolio → Message → Discuss requirements → Negotiate → Share files → Manage revisions → Make payment

The problem wasn't simply the absence of another freelancer marketplace.

The bigger opportunity was to bring the entire Creator–Crew relationship into one structured workflow.

How might we make the Creator–Crew hiring and project lifecycle feel trustworthy, understandable and easy to manage — from discovery through completion?

02 — Goal

Validate the Creator ↔ Crew marketplace for MVP 1

Creator

Find the right person for a project and get the work completed.

Crew

Find relevant opportunities and get hired for projects that match their skills.

The complete lifecycle:

Discover → Evaluate → Post / Apply → Shortlist → Hire → Collaborate → Deliver → Approve → Complete

Rather than designing isolated screens, I treated the lifecycle as the product.

06 — Three Core Product Insights

What shaped the product decisions

Insight 01

The marketplace needed to sell trust, not just talent.

A Creator can't make a good hiring decision from a name and profile picture.

So I treated the Crew profile as a professional hiring profile, rather than a traditional social profile.

Software Expertise

Give Creators enough relevant information to confidently evaluate someone before starting a project.

Insight 02

Discovery and execution are two different product modes.

Before Hiring

Discover → Evaluate → Compare → Shortlist → Hire

→

After Hiring

Plan → Communicate → Deliver → Review → Revise → Approve

Marketplace → Project Workspace

Before hiring, the Creator is making a decision. After hiring, they're managing execution.

Insight 03

Creative work is iterative by nature.

The assumption

Brief → Perfect Output → Done

The reality

Brief → First Delivery → Feedback → Revision → Review → Approval

Revisions couldn't be treated as an afterthought. They had to become part of the core product workflow.

Project creation can require time and thought. Instead of treating an abandoned form as a dead end, the system supports:

Interruption became a recoverable workflow instead of failure.

12 — Designing for the Unhappy Path

A polished happy path doesn't make a robust product.

The main state progression:

Draft → Pending Payment → Active → Applications → Shortlisted → Crew Assigned → In Progress → Under Review → Revision Requested → Completed

Alternate states:

Cancelled Rejected Withdrawn Disputed

Edge cases I considered:

  • Creator leaves during project creation
  • Payment succeeds but user leaves
  • Multiple Crew members attempt to be hired
  • Crew uploads an invalid file
  • Creator doesn't respond to a submission
  • Revision is requested
  • Payment release fails
  • A dispute occurs

The question shifted from "What should the screen look like?" to "What should the product do when the expected thing doesn't happen?"

14 — My Design Approach

Workflow-first, not screen-first.

01

Define the user

Creator or Crew?

02

Define the goal

Hire someone or get hired?

03

Map the workflow

Goal → Actions → Decisions → States → Completion

04

Define the information hierarchy

What should the user know first? What supports the decision? What is the primary action?

05

Design the interaction

Only after the workflow was clear did I move into the interface.

06

Systematize

Apply reusable components and patterns.

07

Think beyond the happy path

Consider alternate states, failures and recovery.

15 — Outcome

A structured marketplace concept

FanCrew MVP 1 evolved into a structured Creator–Crew marketplace concept connecting:

Discovery → Evaluation → Hiring → Collaboration → Delivery → Approval → Payment

I designed the key experiences across both sides of the marketplace while establishing the design-system foundation required to scale the product.

A Creator can start with "I need someone for this project" and move toward "the right person is hired, the work is completed, and the relationship is settled."

16 — What I Learned

Reflections on designing a two-sided marketplace

01

A marketplace is a relationship product.

The value isn't just the listing or profile. It's the relationship that happens after two people find each other.

02

Designing states is as important as designing screens.

The real product appears when users:

leave → return → revise → reject → cancel → dispute → recover

03

Two-sided products require two mental models.

Creators and Crew belong to the same ecosystem but have different jobs and information needs.

04

Design systems are product infrastructure.

Consistency across multiple roles and workflows cannot depend on manually designing every screen.

Final Takeaway

FanCrew taught me to think about product design beyond individual interfaces.

The challenge wasn't:

"How do I design a marketplace?"

It was:

"How do I design an ecosystem where discovery, trust, hiring, collaboration, delivery and payment work together as one coherent experience?"

That shift — from screen design to system design — became the most important part of my work on FanCrew.
